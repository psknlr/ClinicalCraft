## Learning outcomes

1. Reconstruct injection-reaction timing.

4. Explain safety limits of monitored desensitisation.

## Clinical summary

The source treated injection-related hypersensitivity with monitored desensitisation and reported short-term tolerance; marked saline-control wheals prevent the skin-test panel alone from confirming allergy to every insulin. Timing and wheal morphology support hypersensitivity assessment. Both negative repeat serum testing and a reactive saline control require clinical correlation; absolute wheal size alone is not diagnostic. Treatment necessity and safety resources jointly guide planning.

## Decisions and assessment

- **diagnosis:** What do exposure and earlier testing support? Options: Injection-related hypersensitivity needs specialist assessment including formulation components and technique (supported: Immediate wheals align with testing but other contact factors remain relevant.) Wheals within minutes are only lipohypertrophy (distractor: Timing and itching do not fit isolated chronic lipohypertrophy.) Protamine is proven as the sole allergen (distractor: Protamine-specific IgE was negative and the sole component cannot be assigned.)
- **evidence:** Which conclusion is supported by the subsequent records? Options: Later negative specific IgE does not erase the history; marked saline-control wheals prevent this skin-test panel from independently confirming allergy to each formulation (supported: Review controls, initial bleb and reading times alongside exposure reactions. Nonspecific irritation or dermographism are possibilities to assess, not mechanisms established by this table.) Negative IgE permits a full unsupervised home dose (distractor: Negative serum testing does not remove risk from earlier immediate reactions; an uncertain skin-test panel does not license self-challenge.) An absolute wheal diameter above 3 mm confirms allergy to every insulin without considering saline (distractor: The saline control itself reached 12 mm; ignoring it risks misclassifying nonspecific reactivity as drug-specific allergy. This table cannot provide that confirmation.)
- **management:** How should safe management be discussed? Options: Assess insulin necessity and let allergy/endocrine teams select formulation or monitored desensitisation (supported: First review skin-test controls and clinical correlation; desensitisation carries allergic and glycaemic risks, and historical steps are not a home prescription.) Copy every historical escalation dose for self-administration (distractor: A team with monitoring and emergency capability is needed.) Permanently ban all insulin without assessing metabolic need (distractor: This may remove necessary treatment and requires individual assessment.)

## Clinical numbers check

- Earlier allergy testing: Total IgE 211 kU/L, human/porcine/bovine insulin-specific IgE class 2 and protamine class 0, with differing intradermal responses to formulations.
- Repeat testing and formulation comparison: Total IgE was 266 kU/L but insulin and protamine specific IgE were class 0. Table 1 records a saline-control wheal as “+” at ten minutes, 12 mm at thirty minutes, 10 mm at one hour and 6 mm at two hours, resolving at three to four hours; corresponding lispro measurements were 12, 12 and 9 mm. The authors selected lispro from this comparison, but the marked saline response prevents absolute wheal diameter alone from confirming allergy to every formulation. HbA1c was 7.3% and creatinine 118 µmol/L.

Source values and units are retained. Missing reference intervals, weights and timing are not reconstructed. Historical drug doses are omitted from actionable options.
