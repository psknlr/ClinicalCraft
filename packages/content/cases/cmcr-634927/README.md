## Learning outcomes

1. Recognise a hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state from unconsciousness, a glucose of 33.7 mmol/L, no ketosis or acidosis and a high
   osmolality (the JBDS-IP 2022 features), and name what a hyperglycaemic crisis alone does not explain: urine of
   specific gravity 1.005 beside a sodium of 172 mmol/L.
2. Plan the first 24 hours safely: 0.9% sodium chloride first; with a potassium of 3.2 mmol/L, potassium before
   insulin, and insulin only once the glucose stops falling on fluids (0.05 U/kg/h); hourly monitoring; a sodium that
   falls by no more than 10 mmol/L in 24 hours (JBDS-IP 2022; 2024 consensus).
3. When polyuria and hypernatraemia persist after the glucose is controlled, recognise a urine-concentrating defect
   (AVP deficiency or resistance) and confirm it with paired plasma and urine osmolality and desmopressin — not with
   water deprivation in a patient already hypernatraemic (Endocrine Society 2016).
4. Interpret AVP deficiency with an empty sella: imaging shows structure, not function — test the anterior pituitary,
   beginning with an 8–9 am cortisol (Endocrine Society 2016).
5. Manage desmopressin-associated hyponatraemia (hold or reduce, sodium every 6 hours, a rise of no more than 10 mmol/L
   in 24 hours; European hyponatraemia guideline 2014) and prevent it (lowest effective dose, a phase of polyuria at
   least weekly; Endocrine Society 2016).
6. Explain to her in plain words how her diabetes mellitus differs from AVP deficiency (diabetes insipidus), and why
   desmopressin must be neither missed nor overdosed (2022 renaming statement; Society for Endocrinology 2018).

## Clinical summary (spoilers)

**What the report records (1.0.0's extraction).** A 50-year-old woman, whose diabetes had been found a year earlier
and was not monitored regularly, took metformin irregularly. Her initial record noted dry mouth and polydipsia for more
than a year. She was found unconscious in bed three hours before presentation, with convulsions of both arms. On
arrival she was unconscious, pulse 104/min, blood pressure 121/74 mmHg. Glucose 33.7 mmol/L; sodium 172 and potassium
3.2 mmol/L; creatinine 122.9 μmol/L; pH 7.4, bicarbonate 36.5 mmol/L; urine ketones negative, urine specific gravity
1.005 and — discordantly — urine glucose negative; an osmolality of "392" whose printed unit needs checking; head CT
unremarkable. About 9 L of fluid were given on day one, with a urine output of 4.7 L; the next day her sodium was
139 mmol/L. She regained consciousness after treatment. About ten days later her glucose was controlled, but her
sodium had risen again to 162 mmol/L and her urine output was 8.4 L a day; because the urine output did not fit the
change in glucose, further questioning disclosed eighteen years of drinking 5–8 L a day (who gave this history is
not recorded). On a water deprivation and posterior-pituitary extract test that 1.0.0 calls previously completed (when
it was done is not recorded), her urine specific gravity rose from 1.005 to 1.020 and her urine output fell;
MRI showed an empty sella. The report's title names hyperosmolar non-ketotic diabetic coma with empty-sella diabetes
insipidus. Desmopressin brought her urine to 1.4–2.4 L a day, but she became dizzy with a sodium of 122 mmol/L; after
the dose was reduced her sodium was 135 mmol/L and her glucose 5.5–7.4 mmol/L. No longer-term outcome is recorded.

**What the case teaches.** Two emergencies in one patient. The first is a hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS): its
treatment is fluid first, potassium before insulin, and every number lowered slowly — the sodium by no more than
10 mmol/L in 24 hours (JBDS-IP 2022; 2024 ADA/EASD/JBDS-IP/AACE/DTS consensus); the report's fall of 33 mmol/L by the
next day is the historical practice those limits are written to prevent. The second is hidden inside the first: urine
with a specific gravity of 1.005 in a woman with a sodium of 172 mmol/L is inappropriately dilute. Once the glucose is
controlled, persisting polyuria with a sodium of 162 mmol/L is a concentrating defect, not habit drinking (which lowers
the sodium) and not glycosuria. A patient already that hyperosmolar needs no water deprivation (the Endocrine Society
2016 protocol stops thirsting at a plasma osmolality of 295 mOsm/L); she needs paired osmolalities, desmopressin under
close sodium checks and controlled water. With an empty sella, the anterior pituitary must be tested, cortisol first.
Desmopressin then most likely retained water — a sodium of 122 mmol/L — and the safe response is to hold or reduce it and let the
sodium rise slowly (European hyponatraemia guideline 2014). Throughout: AVP deficiency is not diabetes mellitus, and
confusing the two has harmed patients (2022 renaming statement).

### Decisions

All five open only after the evidence they need has been read, and each before the result that answers it (the day-one
record answers decision 2; the test and MRI answer decision 3; the outcome answers decision 5). Option ids name their
content; learners see the options shuffled.

1. **`d1-picture`** (ED; after glucose, electrolytes, pH/bicarbonate, urinalysis and CT are read) — *Which reading
   accounts for all of it?* Key **`hhs-water-loss`**: a hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state, with water lost beyond what
   the glucose explains (glucose ≥30 mmol/L, no significant ketonaemia or acidosis, osmolality ≥320 mOsm/kg — 2 × 172 +
   33.7 = 377.7 before urea: JBDS-IP 2022; but urine of specific gravity 1.005 in a hyperosmolar patient is dilute).
   Distractors: ketoacidosis (needs ketones and acidosis — all three components, 2024 consensus); an ordinary
   hyperosmolar state in which glycosuria explains the dilute urine (an osmotic diuresis raises specific gravity, and
   this urine was even recorded glucose-negative); a primary seizure or stroke (a fit does not produce these numbers;
   the CT was unremarkable, which does not exclude every cause).
2. **`d2-first-day`** (ED; after decision 1) — *Plan for the first 24 hours.* Key **`saline-potassium-slow`**: 0.9%
   saline (2024 consensus: 500–1000 mL/h for 2–4 h without heart or kidney compromise — her creatinine is 122.9 μmol/L
   with no baseline, so reassess often), potassium (below 3.5 mmol/L: senior review and more than the standard
   replacement, JBDS-IP 2022), insulin only once the glucose stops falling on fluids alone (0.05 U/kg/h, JBDS-IP 2022),
   osmolality hourly at first falling 3–8 mOsm/kg an hour, glucose by ≤5 mmol/L an hour and sodium by ≤10 mmol/L in
   24 hours; watch the urine output. Distractors: normalise the sodium today with 5% glucose or 0.45% saline; insulin
   0.1 U/kg/h at once with potassium later; desmopressin now with 5% glucose replacement (diabetes insipidus is not
   established while the glucose is 33.7 mmol/L — the Endocrine Society 2016 criteria need a glucose-negative dipstick —
   and even in known cranial DI the Society for Endocrinology 2018 restores volume with 0.9% saline first and warns
   that desmopressin with large fluid loads corrects hypernatraemia too fast).
3. **`d3-ward-polyuria`** (ward; after the day-one record, the ward review and the drinking history) — *What next?* Key
   **`concentrating-defect`**: controlled water replacement, paired plasma and urine osmolality, desmopressin with close
   sodium checks — no water deprivation. Distractors: a formal deprivation test tomorrow with nil by mouth from midnight
   (a sodium of 162 mmol/L already puts her plasma osmolality past the 295 mOsm/L at which the Endocrine Society 2016
   protocol stops thirsting); restrict her to 1.5 L a day as habit drinking (primary polydipsia lowers the sodium;
   hypernatraemia in cranial DI is a medical emergency for a high-dependency setting, SfE 2018); more glucose-lowering
   treatment and litre-for-litre 0.9% saline (glucose is controlled; 0.9% saline carries 154 mmol/L of sodium).
4. **`d4-test-scan`** (ward; after the test record and the MRI report, both released by decision 3) — *What do these
   mean, and what comes before long-term desmopressin?* Key **`avp-deficiency-pituitary`**: kidneys that concentrate
   fit AVP deficiency (central DI), the empty sella goes with it but proves no cause, and the anterior pituitary is
   tested — an 8–9 am cortisol first (Endocrine Society 2016 1.1), free T4 with TSH (1.6). The feedback says plainly
   that the record does not separate a rise during thirsting from one after the extract. Distractors: the empty sella
   proves panhypopituitarism, so start hydrocortisone, levothyroxine and desmopressin untested (test first; assess
   adrenal function before levothyroxine, ES 2016 2.20); nephrogenic DI with a thiazide (AVP resistance stays below
   300 mOsm/kg after desmopressin in the ES 2016 protocol); discharge on desmopressin with a sodium check weeks later
   (anterior pituitary testing first; ES 2016 2.15 education and a weekly phase of polyuria; early sodium checks).
5. **`d5-low-sodium`** (on desmopressin; after the record and asking how she feels) — *Sodium 122 mmol/L with dizziness.*
   Key **`hold-correct-slowly`**: hold or reduce the desmopressin, drink only to thirst, sodium every 6 hours, a rise of
   ≤10 mmol/L in the first 24 hours (and ≤8 in each 24 hours after), then restart lower (European hyponatraemia
   guideline 2014: 7.4.1.1, 7.4.1.4, 7.4.1.5; an undocumented duration is treated as chronic, 6.1.2.3; Endocrine Society
   2016 2.15). Distractors: repeated 150 mL boluses of 3% saline until +5 mmol/L with desmopressin unchanged (the
   severe-symptom protocol, 7.1; dizziness is neither a severe nor a moderately severe symptom); same dose with salt
   tablets and plenty of fluid; stop desmopressin for good with free drinking and no checks (risk of over-rapid rise and
   osmotic demyelination; her AVP deficiency still needs treatment).

decision 4 only (56 characters each) and is never uniquely longest.

lose `rb-no-sodium-harm`. Timing is scored
where it matters: fluids, potassium and monitoring count only while she is still in the emergency department, water
replacement only on the ward before the outcome is read, and the desmopressin change only before the outcome is read

before a decision name the harm and the guidance without listing that decision's whole answer.

**Safety outcome.** Three interventions are `critical: true` (high risk), because giving them while contraindicated is
this case's dangerous error with life-threatening harm: an **insulin infusion in the emergency department** with a
potassium of 3.2 mmol/L and no fluid resuscitation yet (hypokalaemic arrhythmia, a precipitous fall in osmolality and
circulatory collapse — JBDS-IP 2022); a **fast hypotonic infusion** to normalise the sodium (cerebral oedema and osmotic
demyelination — the ≤10 mmol/L in 24 hours limit of JBDS-IP 2022 and the 2024 consensus); and a **water deprivation
test** in a patient whose sodium is 162–172 mmol/L (the Endocrine Society 2016 protocol stops thirsting at 295 mOsm/L).
Each fails the run's safety outcome whatever the score and costs the critical item `rb-no-unsafe-correction`.
Desmopressin given while contraindicated, 3% saline, controlled water in the emergency phase or at a sodium of
122 mmol/L, and fluid restriction on the ward are moderate risk and `critical: false` — recoverable misjudgements that
the next clinician can stop and correct — and cost `rb-no-sodium-harm`. Only an order to run a hypotonic fluid fast or
to normalise the sodium reaches the critical `rapid-hypotonic`; a measured hypotonic fluid (5% glucose, 0.45% saline,
in any route) reaches `free-water`. No other intervention is high risk.

mmol/L/h, sodium ≤10 mmol/L/24 h, osmolality ≤3.0–8.0 mOsm/kg/h; HHS without significant ketonaemia: insulin
0.05 U/kg/h (withholding it until glucose stops falling is reported as what "some have recommended"); DKA insulin 0.1 U/kg/h; in DKA with K <3.5, delay
insulin until K >3.5. SfE 2018 (cranial DI): hypernatraemia severe >160 mmol/L, a medical emergency for level 2–3 care;
0.9% saline first if volume depleted; sodium every 4 h during resuscitation; correction ≤0.5 mmol/L/h and ≤10 mmol/L/24 h
with no or mild symptoms; desmopressin IV/IM 1–2 µg, oral or sublingual 100–200 µg, intranasal 10–20 µg; 0.9% saline
154 mmol/L sodium. ES 2016: polyuria >50 mL/kg/24 h; plasma >295 mOsm/L → urine ~600 mOsm/L (ratio ≥2) with a
glucose-negative dipstick; deprivation protocol stops at plasma osmolality >295 mOsm/L with dilute urine; after
desmopressin >800 mOsm/kg central, <300 mOsm/kg nephrogenic. ESE 2014: profound hyponatraemia <125 mmol/L; rise
≤10 mmol/L in the first 24 h and ≤8 in each 24 h after; sodium every 6 h; 150 mL 3% saline over 20 min for severe
symptoms (repeat to +5 mmol/L) or once for moderately severe symptoms or a documented acute fall >10 mmol/L.

### Common learner errors and how the case surfaces them

| Error | How the case surfaces it |
|---|---|
| Calling it ketoacidosis because she is unconscious with a high glucose | decision 1 distractor; the urinalysis and pH/bicarbonate are read before it opens |
| Starting insulin at once (often at 0.1 U/kg/h) | decision 2 distractor; the `insulin` intervention is critical in the ED phase |
| Chasing a normal sodium on day one | decision 2 distractor; `rapid-hypotonic` is critical; the day-one record shows the report's 33 mmol/L fall |
| Reading the dilute urine as part of the osmotic diuresis | decision 1 distractor; the attending's question on specific gravity |
| Habit drinking, or a water deprivation test, for hypernatraemic polyuria | decision 3 distractors; `water-deprivation` is critical |
| Treating the empty sella as proof of hypopituitarism, or as irrelevant | decision 4 distractors; cortisol ordering is part of `rb-d4` |
| Continuing desmopressin or reaching for 3% saline at 122 mmol/L | decision 5 distractors; both acts are contraindicated and cost `rb-no-sodium-harm` |
| Restricting fluids for "habit drinking" on the ward; water by mouth or a hypotonic first fluid while she is unconscious | decision 3 distractor; `fluid-restriction` is contraindicated on the ward and `free-water` in the emergency phase and at sodium 122 (each costs `rb-no-sodium-harm`) |
| Forgetting the routine safety of HHS care (monitoring, potassium, LMWH, senior review) | `rb-first-hours` (critical) and `rb-admission` |

3. 血糖控制后多尿和高钠仍持续时识别尿液浓缩障碍，用配对的血、尿渗透压和去氨加压素证实，不对高钠患者做禁水试验（美国内分泌学会2016）。
4. 解读AVP缺乏合并空蝶鞍：检查垂体前叶功能，先查晨8–9时皮质醇（美国内分泌学会2016）。

项：明早禁水试验；按习惯性多饮限水；加强降糖并按尿量补0.9%氯化钠）；④ 试验与影像：符合AVP缺乏，空蝶鞍相关但不能证明病因，先查晨
皮质醇等垂体前叶功能（干扰项：空蝶鞍即全垂体功能减退、不查就同时补三种激素；肾性尿崩症；偶然发现、出院几周后再查血钠）；⑤ 血钠
