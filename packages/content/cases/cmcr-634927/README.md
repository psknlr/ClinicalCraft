## Learning outcomes

1. Recognise severe water loss within a hyperosmolar presentation.
2. Investigate a second mechanism when polyuria persists after glucose control.
3. Distinguish empty-sella imaging from functional diagnosis.
4. Explain sodium safety during fluid and antidiuretic therapy.

## Clinical summary

The report describes hyperosmolar hyperglycaemia with central diabetes-insipidus physiology and an empty sella; subsequent desmopressin-associated hyponatraemia highlights monitoring. Severe hypernatraemia with dilute urine requires assessment of water regulation. Persistent abnormalities after glucose improvement challenge a glycosuria-only explanation. Imaging associations and historical treatment responses have limits.

## Decisions and assessment

- **diagnosis:** Which emergency framework best fits the initial metabolic findings? Options: A hyperosmolar state with hyperglycaemia and severe hypernatraemia, also requiring investigation of water loss (supported: Ketoacidosis is unsupported, and dilute urine must not be ignored.) Coma with hyperglycaemia necessarily means ketoacidosis (distractor: The pH, bicarbonate and urine ketones do not support it.) Normal head CT allows sodium and glucose to be ignored (distractor: Severe metabolic disturbance can itself threaten neurological function.)
- **evidence:** Which conclusion is supported by the subsequent records? Options: Persistent dilute polyuria and hypernatraemia after glucose control, with hormone responsiveness, support central water-regulation deficiency; empty sella is an associated clue (supported: Imaging association is not proof of a sole cause; full pituitary assessment is missing.) All urine loss can still be attributed to glycosuria after glucose normalises (distractor: Persistent urine and sodium abnormalities need an independent explanation.) Empty sella proves deficiency of every pituitary hormone (distractor: Structural imaging does not replace functional assessment.)
- **management:** Which safety problem needs particular attention in current care? Options: Closely monitor sodium, urine output, volume and glucose, coordinating fluids with desmopressin to avoid rapid correction and hyponatraemia (supported: Do not copy the historical fall from 172 to 139 in one day or a fixed fluid volume.) Use the source first-day sodium fall as the target (distractor: A historical change is not a safe correction target.) Ask the patient to self-deprive water during severe hypernatraemia (distractor: Further water restriction can worsen dangerous water loss.)
