## Clinical summary

The source describes an albendazole-associated severe bullous drug eruption with insufficient evidence for precise modern SJS/TEN classification. Skin detachment establishes urgency. The multi-drug timeline affects causality. Missing area and mucosal data limit classification.

## Decisions and assessment

- **diagnosis:** What is the most important clinical framing? Options: A severe epidermal-barrier emergency, with drug reaction differentiated from other paediatric blistering disorders (supported: Detachment exceeds an ordinary urticarial reaction.) Itching proves only mild allergy (distractor: Bullae and skin loss are severe warning signs.) TEN can be precisely classified without area data (distractor: SJS/TEN classification needs actual surface area and clinical information.)
- **evidence:** Which conclusion is supported by the subsequent records? Options: The first-drug timeline supports suspicion of albendazole, but multiple drugs and missing pathology limit causality and classification (supported: Later drugs cannot explain initial onset but remain relevant to subsequent assessment.) Re-exposure is required for safe confirmation (distractor: A severe skin reaction must not be confirmed through unprotected rechallenge.) No previous allergy excludes drug eruption (distractor: A first severe reaction can still occur.)
- **management:** What should safe current care emphasise? Options: Stop suspected non-essential medicines and obtain urgent dermatology/paediatric assessment of barrier, ocular/mucosal, fluid and infection risks (supported: Reduce skin friction; immune therapy and antibiotics require specialist assessment and actual infection evidence.) Repeatedly rub skin to confirm Nikolsky sign (distractor: This can enlarge epidermal damage.) Use historical fixed high-dose steroids and prophylactic antibiotics for every child (distractor: A single historical regimen is not a universal current protocol.)
